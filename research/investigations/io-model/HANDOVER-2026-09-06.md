## 2. The chain of motivation, batch by batch

The branch carries three batches. Each exists because the previous one
could not settle a question.

### 2.1 Batch 1 (record `archive/done/0106-backed-file-permit.md`): the backed file permit

Under v0.41 a `FilePermit` promised nothing the host could run out of. A
close produced nothing the checker could see, so a staged pipeline
overlapped it with a later open, the host answered `EMFILE` for a schedule
the sequential program never produces, and the runtime hid that with a
process-wide descriptor retirement ledger (waiters, award order,
retire-and-retry, a blocking direct call holding its thread while another
engine gave a descriptor back). The owner's ruling of 2026-09-04,
constitution T4, named that apparatus as the symptom of one missing API
relation. The batch put the relation on the API:

- `reserve_file` (later `reserve_handle`) answers `Result<Permit, IoError>`
  from a factory whose capacity is the target's; the permit value is the
  credit; the count is never raised.
- The four opens answer with their own outcome enums whose failed variant
  hands the permit back beside the host's error (owner decision: "the error
  is the system's, so the open keeps it; the permit is ours, so it comes back
  where the checker can see it").
- Three explicit closes return the credit as a fresh permit.
- The retirement ledger and its probes were deleted on every route and
  target.

Three things the implementation revealed about the first text, all
recorded in that record's §7: explicit close counted the credit twice (fixed
by making the permit value the credit); an unspent permit's release must
spend the credit, not raise a count (T4); a failed open's credit comes back
in the outcome, not to the factory (owner decision). Do not reintroduce a
count that goes up.

### 2.2 Batch 2 (record `archive/done/0107-park-on-miss.md`, design `research/investigations/io-model/PARK-ON-MISS.md`): park on miss, one scheduler core

Before it, overlap came from two machines: `par_runtime.c` for compute
hand-outs and `completion/runtime.c` for completions, the latter through a
token into 256 slots with a capacity wait, a refusal path back to a blocking
direct call, a writer scheduler beside it, and a stackless continuation
lowering in the emitter. Each was a bound the source could exceed. The
design's principle: no completed operation's continuation may be buried
behind something that is waiting. The answer: one scheduler for both kinds
of hand-out, a join that parks its own stack when it misses, a completion
record that lives in the submitting frame (no pool to size, refuse from, or
index).

What was built and how it was gated:

- `compiler/src/backend/sched/`: `core.c/h` (the protocol), `prim.h` (seven
  primitives, two implementations: `prim_host.c` and the enumerator's),
  `switch.h` (one stack switch), `entry.c` (thread pool, settings, report),
  `grant_observer.c` (test-only, prints `grants=` and the `sched:` and
  `ring:` counter lines under `WF_SCHED_REPORT=1`).
- The gate is the enumerator (`enumerate.c`, `schedules.c`, wrapper
  `backend/tests/sched.rs`, `sched-enumerate` under `completion-test`): one
  OS thread, simulated threads as coroutines, explicit-state search with
  every §11 invariant checked after every step, at (T=1,S=2), (T=1,S=3),
  (T=2,S=3), (T=2,S=4). It found six defects in the core before any real
  thread ran (entry thread's last pool stack unresumable; a late publisher
  acting on a cancelled park, fixed by the claim protocol; publisher
  touching a dead record, fixed by PENDING/COMPLETING/DONE; the in-place
  waiter sleeping past another thread's drain; the compute arm never
  draining; a worker start finding no stack).
- Emitter: `compute_join_order` decides join order once (compute newest
  first, completions where published); the record is an opaque frame block
  of `WF_COMPLETION_RECORD_BYTES` (160); the stackless lowering, the direct
  family and the writer-frame submit ABI were deleted.
- Runtime: the core replaces both runtimes and both writer schedulers; joins
  park; the floor entry runs on a pool stack. Two defects the runtime step
  exposed: a lost wake (the epoch seam had two owners until the bridge
  initialized; fixed with one `pthread_once`) and a ring destroyed under a
  sleeping worker (shutdown no longer destroys the ring while the pool
  runs). A lost wake is a hang by design; there is no timeout to hide one.
- Windows took seven rounds on the real host after cross-compile and Wine
  were clean: MSVC deprecates `getenv` under `-Werror`; the host runtime must
  not reach the bridge; binding a handle to the port is one critical section;
  destructors run after streams are torn down (use `atexit`). Lesson: a
  cross compiler and an emulator say nothing about the host's C runtime,
  lock discipline, or teardown order.

The §12 measurements (`research/experiments/park-on-miss-measurements/`,
`run.sh`, `make -C compiler park-on-miss-measurements`), one line each:

- §12.1 park and resume at a compute miss: bar missed. `par_layout` 45
  percent slower at four workers, 103 percent at eight, against the
  pre-batch runtime; the grid loop-split program pays nothing (26 parks a run
  against 18 000). The idle spin addendum later narrowed this (see §7 of this
  document: at landing the gap is 8 to 9 percent at four and eight workers).
  Still the owner's open decision. The design's stated fallback (nested runs
  of never-suspends jobs at a miss) needs a target-action bit at the
  hand-out that the emitter does not mark; the variant built without it is a
  live-lock at one thread (enumerator schedule S23). Do not rebuild that
  variant without the bit.
- §12.2 one park and one publish: 4.40 µs best, 6.23 median, against the
  design's 2.2 and this host's own 16.2.
- §12.3 the claim protocol's price: not separated from noise; removing it
  reproduces the S3 fault on a real host in under a second. Keep it.
- §12.4 the in-place wait and the four-stage chain: the chain bar is
  untestable on this host (reads do not wait enough).
- §12.5 memory orders: the weaker orders pass the enumerator at every
  configuration and hang `par_layout` deterministically at two workers and
  above (the store-then-load pair on both sides of §6). Do not weaken them.
- §12.5 stack count: refusals cost no wall time; default threads + 8.
- §12.6 lane slot count: not separated between 4 and 64; two slots cost 12
  percent. Later raised to 1024 for the network test (see 2.3).
- Five of the six behavioural switches could not be measured: four are
  rejected by the enumerator, the fifth hangs a corpus program. The design's
  gate worked as intended.

Open from batch 2 and still open: the compute-miss decision; the GenMC run
(deferred; the enumerator's model is sequentially consistent); two enumerator
gaps named, not closed: it cannot judge the locked fallback of §6, and it
models a fixed set of words, so a per-thread ready list is unmodelled. The
second gap matters for the next PR (see §10).

### 2.3 Batch 3 (record `archive/done/0108-streams-and-tcp.md`, design `research/investigations/io-model/NETWORK.md`): streams and TCP, the first API that waits

Batch 2 could not settle its own numbers because nothing in the language
waited: a cached read is a memory copy and a cold read waits briefly and
uniformly. The owner's revision of 2026-09-05 made the network the first
waiting API, with standard input as the first instance of the same stream
design, because concurrency's real demand is the network and a loopback is a
controlled peer.

Owner decisions (NETWORK.md §8), in force: the network first, standard input
as the first stream; `InputStream` and `OutputStream`; TCP only, address
literals only, no UDP, no name resolution; backlog target-fixed; full duplex
designed in from the start as two halves; the reference for the control test
is the fastest existing solution regardless of language, because the target
is first place. Second round: `HandleFactory`, `HandlePermit`,
`command.handles`, `reserve_handle`, renaming the batch-1 surface across the
corpus in the same amendment (a listener and a connection draw one credit
each from the capacity a file open draws from); `TcpConnection` is a
system-declared struct of `TcpReceive` and `TcpSend`, the first such struct,
so the pairing is on the API and no split or join operation exists; the
seven operation names of NETWORK.md §4.

What landed:

- The amendment: SYS-15 (`InputStream`, entry ordinal 5, `read_next`), SYS-16
  (`SocketAddress`, two total pure constructors), SYS-17 (`TcpListener`,
  `tcp_listen`, `tcp_accept`, `tcp_connect`, `close_listener`), SYS-18
  (`TcpConnection`, `receive_next`, `send_once`, `close_connection`).
  `TcpConnection`'s fields are ordinary places, so two `&uniq` loans on
  disjoint fields coexist under OWN-5 and a partial move kills the whole
  binding under OWN-1. Eleven conformance cases added, thirty-one respelled,
  five `systcp-*` verdicts moved from `unsupported` to `accept` when the
  subject became supported (the expectation about the language did not
  change; the subject moved).
- Two compiler defects the amendment exposed: lowering decoded a system
  constructor ordinal against the active inventory instead of the unit's own
  (caught by the prefix-differential property); no checked borrow form
  carried a field path for a non-buffer type (`&uniq connection.receive`
  needed one).
- POSIX runtime: `file_posix.c` executes every kind; `linux_io_uring.c`
  carries accept, connect, receive and send on the ring (a connect's socket
  and native address are made in the submitting call, because neither can
  wait); listen, bind and half-close stay on the adapter; `file_adapter.c`
  keeps the pair's two-count so the second release closes the object and
  spends the credit. `SO_REUSEADDR` is deliberately not set: two binds of one
  port are the program's own source-order conflict.
- Windows runtime: the six operations reach the same two engines in shared
  code; `ConnectEx`, `WSARecv`, `WSASend` on the record's own `OVERLAPPED`;
  the accept stays on the adapter because `AcceptEx`'s 88-byte address pair
  has nowhere to live in a 160-byte record and the runtime allocates nothing.
- The staged hand-out of a may-suspend user call (`loops.rs`
  `StagedTail::Bound`, `may_suspend_staged_call`): a fixed-trip loop whose
  body is one user call that may suspend is staged to a compute lane; that is
  what lets the echo server keep one parked callee per connection.
- The control benchmark bundle in `research/experiments/io-completion-bench/`:
  `uring_echo.c` (raw io_uring ABI, multishot accept and receive into a
  registered buffer ring, one ring and one `SO_REUSEPORT` listener per
  thread, `SINGLE_ISSUER|DEFER_TASKRUN`, sendmsg out of the kernel-filled
  buffer), `epoll_echo.c` (one edge-triggered epoll and listener per thread),
  `netload.c` (one load generator: opens every connection, drives all at
  once, verifies every echoed byte, reports round trips per second and the
  latency distribution; four threads by default), `linux-net-bench.sh` (warm-up
  and recorded passes in alternating order, medians; ports below the
  ephemeral range; readiness via `/proc/net/tcp`), `programs/tcp_echo_server.wf`
  (`wf_echo PORT CONNECTIONS`, a fixed-trip accept loop over the connection
  count, one parked callee per connection), Makefile targets `net-tools
  net-verify linux-net`, and the "Run the Linux network protocol" step in
  `io-bench.yml` (ROUNDS 3, timeout 12 minutes).

Defects the batch found and fixed, with the lesson of each:

- macOS harness accept published the zero address: the family was read as a
  sixteen-bit number at the record's first byte, which is `sa_family` on
  Linux and `sa_len` beside a one-byte `sa_family` on Darwin. Copy the host's
  own `struct sockaddr` and read the member (`socket_address.h`).
- The Windows echo server never listened: the four programs copied the port
  argument with `host_copy_bytes`, the lossless route, which on a UTF-16 host
  hands the program native code units, so "60527" arrived as `36 00 30 00`.
  Read text arguments through `host_copy_utf8`. The io-hosts step now lists
  the process's own TCP endpoints when a server does not listen.
- A stall at exactly 129 connections: a depth-64 ring's completion queue
  holds 128 entries; the 129th completion went to the kernel's overflow list
  under `IORING_FEAT_NODROP`, only an `io_uring_enter` moves it into the
  queue, no submission was coming because every callee was parked on one of
  those completions, and the descriptor stays readable for exactly that
  reason, so every park returned at once and four threads spun reaping
  nothing. Fix: map the submission ring's flags word, treat
  `IORING_SQ_CQ_OVERFLOW` as a non-empty queue in the park, enter for no
  minimum inside the non-waiting progress pass while the flag is raised,
  size the completion queue by the caller (`IORING_SETUP_CQSIZE`, 2048 for
  the bridge, 16 in the probe whose new case reaps 24 reads past a 16-entry
  queue).
- The adapter route (Darwin, and `WF_IO_NO_NATIVE_RING` on Linux) holds at 64
  connections: `wf__completion_window` caps a staged loop at
  `WF_BRIDGE_MAX_HELPERS` (8) when no ring is the engine, so the server keeps
  eight connections open, the generator closes nothing until every
  connection has finished, the ninth accept is never issued. Documented, not
  a socket bug; the readiness-driven adapter (poll/kqueue/WSAPoll over the
  queued descriptors inside the park) is the proper engine and is a separate
  PR. Peer-bound requests (accept, receive, connect, send) always grow a
  helper on the adapter route and are never claimed by a scheduler thread's
  own progress pass while a helper exists (`wf_file_request_is_peer_bound`).
- The macOS gate's reset test raced: the peer closed as soon as its payload
  was written; on the macOS runner the close ran ahead of the echo, the
  receive queue held nothing at the close, the host sent a graceful end, the
  program read `ReadEnd` and exited 0. A receive the submitting thread
  answers at once made the race real. The peer now peeks one echoed byte
  before it closes. `WF_WORKERS=1` is the opt-out (no lanes, the loop is not
  staged, the program serves one connection to its end before the next
  accept); a client that drives all connections at once waits on the first.
  That is the sequential program's semantics, not a hang.
- Constants raised for the test: `WF_SCHED_LANE_SLOTS 1024`,
  `WF_SCHED_MAX_STACKS 2048`, `LANE_SLOTS = 1024` (exported from the crate
  root for the program test), `WF_BRIDGE_WINDOW_DEFAULT 1024`,
  `WF_HARNESS_WINDOW_DEFAULT 1024`; the protocol sets `WF_STACKS=1100`. A
  Darwin pin that asserted no `[1024 x` array in the module broke because
  `PATH_MAX` is 1024 there; it now pins the lane ring's own shape.

E19 on the protocol (host after restart, ratios only): skipping the host
attempt when the half's poll is armed and has reported nothing reads 0.94 at
one connection (baseline 1.26), 0.72 at 64 (0.76), 0.75 at 1024 (0.64), 0.96
on 64 KiB (0.82); always attempting first reads 1.30, 0.77, 0.63, 0.84,
which is the baseline within noise. A four-hundred-line lock-free slot
protocol that trades one connection for a thousand was not kept. The full
design and its protocol (the Dekker pair between `waiter` and `readable`,
the generation for stale completions, the remove at the half's release so
the host's close is a close) is written out in
`research/investigations/io-model/RESULTS.md` (third series) so it can be
rebuilt if a thousand connections becomes the target. Do not rebuild it for
the 64-connection line.

The stage dwell of one waiting receive, timed (not shipped) on d016afb: 70
µs from staging to the kick, 305 µs from the kick to the reap, 95 µs from
the publication to the stack running again, about 470 µs in all, against a
60 µs client turnaround. Every stage is a queue: the receive waits for the
current batch of stacks to drain before anyone reaches the ring, the
completion waits for the next pass, the resumed stack waits behind the batch
in the ready list. Taking the kick out (E19), moving it to a kernel thread
(E20) or splitting it four ways (E18) left the rate where it was: the batch
cycle, not the kick, is the period. The series-two claim that the kick was
"22 percent of the wall time" was serial work the cycle hid, not paid for.

### 3.5 What `perf` says (the reason, with data)

`linux-tools-generic` installs perf on this Ubuntu 24.04 host even though
the kernel is 6.18; call the binary directly:
`/usr/lib/linux-tools/6.8.0-139-generic/perf record -e cpu-clock -F 4000 -g`.

At 64 connections the Whitefoot server spends 20 percent of samples in
`_raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore` and 9 in `finish_task_switch`; the reference
spends 14 and 14. 17.8 of the 20 are under the program's own `send`: the
loopback delivery wakes the client's thread (`tcp_data_ready`,
`sock_def_readable`, `__wake_up_sync_key`) and both servers pay it in full.
The runtime's own symbols at 64 connections: `pthread_mutex_lock` 2.8
percent, the progress pass, the completion and the submit about 1 each; the
kernel's TCP path is the rest.

At 4 connections the picture is different and is where the line loses most:
`finish_task_switch` 21 percent, `pthread_mutex_lock` 8, `wf_sched_find`
(the steal scan) 3.7, `wf_prim_pause` 3.2; 65 to 70 thousand round trips a
second against the reference's 294 thousand, on 4.5 CPU-seconds against
0.76. Four scheduler threads chase four connections through one ready list
and one lock and switch context once or twice per round trip; the
reference's four threads each own one connection and switch once, on the
arrival of their own data.

CPU accounting over one run of 128 thousand round trips at 64 connections
(bash `time`; `/usr/bin/time` is not installed): uring 0.72 wall / 0.03 user
/ 0.69 sys; epoll 0.79 / 0.05 / 0.75; wf 0.90 / 0.17 / 1.00; netload against
epoll 0.51 wall, 0.69 sys; against wf 0.66 wall, 0.76 sys. Nothing is
saturated. Pinning the server to two cores and the client to the other two
changes nothing (uring 316k, wf 198k, epoll 285k), and with two dedicated
cores wf is flat at about 210k whatever the worker count, so on two cores it
is CPU-bound at about 9 µs per trip against the reference's 2.5.

Conclusion recorded in `mcts_mem/whitefoot/system-interface.md`: the
remaining margin is the scheduler's shape (one ready list, one lock, stack
migration between threads), not the ring. The design that answers it is a
connection that stays on the thread that reaped it, a per-thread ready
list, a steal only from an idle thread. It is a core change the enumerator
has to cover (the enumerator models a fixed word set; a per-thread ready
list is unmodelled, batch 2 §9), and it is the next PR.

### 3.6 Measurement pitfalls that cost time

- The host is a VM and restarted mid-session (uptime went from ten hours to
  thirty minutes). After the restart the same binary measured in two states
  at 64 connections, 180 to 195 thousand and 255 to 275 thousand, baseline
  and candidates alike when run alternately, and the references moved by a
  fifth between protocol runs. Read only ratios within one run; alternate
  A and B; take the runner's table as the check. The earlier "two steady
  states" (95k against 36k before E1) never reappeared after E1 but this
  bimodality did.
- `strace` changes the regime: under it nearly every receive finds its data
  already there, so counts of enters and parks taken under strace describe a
  different program. `--seccomp-bpf -e trace=...` on a subset still halves
  the rate. Use the runtime's own counters (`WF_SCHED_REPORT=1`) and perf.
- Random ports in 48000 to 56000 overlap the ephemeral range; a server bind
  collides with a client's `TIME_WAIT` socket from the previous trial a few
  percent of the time and the trial reads as "parks=0, no rate". Use 20000
  to 30000 (the protocol already uses ports below the ephemeral range).
- `pkill -f` matching the shell's own command line kills the shell (exit
  144); `pgrep -f` matching itself hangs a waiter. Match on the binary name.
- The `exp.sh` build takes `EXTRA_CFLAGS` from the environment; a run
  without it silently measures the baseline (one whole set of W=2/W=8
  results had to be redone).
- Three `tests/programs` cases fail as root (`chmod 000` is no barrier);
  they pass in CI's non-root jobs. The corpus stage's cargo invocation stops
  at the first failing binary, so the `snapshot` integration target after
  `programs` must be run on its own when working as root.
- `make check` stops at the first failing stage; run stages separately and
  report each (this is now a repository rule).
- `research-tests` needs `cargo fetch --locked` for the research manifests
  before `--offline` works on a fresh host.

## 7. Do not retry (with the reason)

- Reap budget above 64 (E7): same as 64.
- Reading the staged count without the lock (E2), publishing after dropping
  the completion lock (E3): no effect; the locks are not what threads wait
  on.
- Spin variants (E4, E8, E26): the spin holds no completion and is not the
  cost at 64; at 4 connections removing it makes the park path pay instead
  (sys time rises).
- `io_uring_enter` per submission (E9) or at a threshold (E15): syscalls per
  operation are unaffordable; the deferred doorbell is right.
- Enter outside the submission lock (E10): no effect.
- `POLL_FIRST` (E16): no measurable effect.
- Reaping during the busy phase (E17): no effect; the batch cycle is the
  period.
- A ring per thread (E18, E18b): no gain at four threads on four cores;
  enters triple.
- `SQPOLL` (E20): worse; a poll thread competes with the client for cores.
- Deferred wake (E25): no effect.
- `EPOLLEXCLUSIVE` (E29): within noise.
- Armed multishot poll per receive half (E19): trades k1 for k1024; not
  worth 400 lines for the 64-connection line; design preserved in RESULTS.md.
- Never-suspends nested run at a compute miss without the emitter's
  target-action bit: live-lock at one thread (S23).
- Weaker memory orders in the core: pass the enumerator, hang `par_layout`.
- Removing the claim protocol: reproduces S3 on a real host.
- Sleeping at once in the in-place wait: fails S23's coverage.
- A count that goes back up on a permit's release or a failed open (T4).
- Reading arguments on Windows through `host_copy_bytes` when text is meant.
- Judging a Windows change by cross-compile and Wine alone.

## 10. The recommended next PR: the scheduler's shape

What the data says to build, and how to judge it:

1. Keep a connection on the thread that reaped its completion: the reaper
   resumes the woken stack itself (it is idle, it holds the completion) or
   pushes it to its own per-thread ready list, and other threads steal from
   that list only when their own is empty. The global ready list and its one
   lock go away; the core's pool list can stay under the lock or become
   per-thread too.
2. Bring the enumerator along first: the model has a fixed word set and a
   per-thread ready list is unmodelled (batch 2 §9). Add the words and the
   §11 invariants for them (a ready stack is on exactly one list; a steal
   is a transfer, never a copy; the lost-wake argument of §6 with a
   per-thread epoch or the shared one) before the host implementation, as
   batch 2 did. The enumerator found six defects before any thread ran; it
   will find the seventh here.
3. Measure with the same protocol and the same counters, one variable at a
   time, alternating with the baseline, on this host in one run. The bars:
   the 4-connection line (65k to 70k today against 294k) should move first
   and most, because the profile says the churn is there; the 64-connection
   line's p50 of 245 µs should fall toward the reference's 60; the compute
   line must not lose what it has (par_layout at four and eight workers).
4. Expect the reference's shape as the ceiling: one thread per connection
   with no cross-thread traffic. The runtime cannot pin a connection to a
   thread by construction (a stack is any thread's), so affinity is a
   scheduling preference, and the measurement will say how much of the gap
   preference recovers.
5. Do not spend time on the ring again; series three closed it.

Second and third candidates for later PRs, in the owner's order: the
data-terminated server loop (language work under PAR-3), the
readiness-driven adapter for ring-less hosts, the compute-miss colouring.
